KIMONO / A CLOSER LOOK

Kimono Patterns: What Is Seen, What Is Hidden

A small plum branch appears near the hem. Where does it continue? One kimono offers a way to look at patterns and the folds that conceal them.

2026.11.08 / KOTO

01 / A BRANCH AT THE HEM

Let your eye follow a small branch

Look at the gray kimono from a little distance: its wide sleeves, the fabric extending to the hem. Near the bottom are a slender branch and small blossoms. If you opened the image expecting an elaborate pattern, it may seem restrained. Yet the number of flowers visible need not determine your impression of the whole garment.

Follow one branch with your eye. Where does it appear, which way does it travel, and where does it disappear? Does the pattern end when it reaches the edge of the cloth? Looking at the overlapping fabric as well as the shapes upon it can bring a small question into view.

02 / ONE KIMONO, ONE RECORD

More plum blossoms beyond the overlap

The photograph shows the Metropolitan Museum of Art’s Kimono with Blossoming Plum Tree, dated to the second half of the nineteenth century. Its record lists plain-weave silk, resist dyeing, ink, pigments, and embroidery in silk and metallic thread. More than one technique contributes to the plum design.

According to the museum, the left panel overlapped the right, concealing much of the plum pattern until the wearer walked. Another plum tree appears on the lining of the inner underflap. The small branch visible at the hem is only part of the design.

This single photograph does not let us inspect the inner pattern or see the garment in motion. It helps to distinguish what we can see from what the museum’s description tells us. We can enjoy imagining the rest without asking one photograph to show more than it does.

03 / LOOK AGAIN

The pattern, and the space around it

The V&A’s introduction to kimono describes nature, including flowers, birds, water, and snow, as a rich source of motifs. But there are things to notice before identifying a flower. Look, for instance, at the gray fabric surrounding the branch in this photograph. Returning your gaze to that area may change how the small blossoms appear.

Here is KOTO’s suggestion for looking. Begin with the whole kimono fitting on your screen. Move closer to the hem and compare the branch’s line with the edges of the cloth. Then return to the whole garment and notice where the decoration gathers. Leave unresolved any details the image cannot reveal, and remember that colors on a screen are not definitive.

04 / KEEP A QUESTION

Take away a question, not just a name

When you next look at a kimono, you might ask not only what a motif means, but where it is placed and what disappears when the fabric overlaps. If a pattern catches your interest, the holding museum’s records are a useful place to check its name and background.

There is no need to turn this garment’s features into rules for every kimono. Between its appearance in a still photograph and its appearance when worn, something remains unseen. Leaving room for that difference can be a beginning to learning about kimono.

TEA / OBJECTS IN CONVERSATION

Toriawase: New Company for Familiar Objects

Tea gatherings offer a way to notice relationships between objects. Before buying something new, look at two familiar pieces together.

2026.11.10 / KOTO

01 / A DIFFERENT NEIGHBOUR

Give a familiar object new company

A favourite bowl can look different without changing at all. Move it from beside a white plate to a wooden tray and look again. Before buying anything, spend a little time with the objects you already have.

Would you repeat a colour or contrast two shapes? Looking at things together may reveal something you missed when considering each one alone. Toriawase, the selection and combination of utensils for a Japanese tea gathering, offers a starting point for this way of looking.

02 / CHOOSING FOR SOMEONE

A choice made with guests in mind

The Asian Art Museum in San Francisco explains that a tea host considers the season, theme and guests when choosing utensils, flowers and a hanging scroll. Displayed objects can also give host and guests something to discuss.

The interesting question becomes more than “What is this called?” or “What does it cost?” Why choose this combination, today, for this person? Perhaps you like it, or it recalls a place you both know. Naming one reason can turn attention to objects into attention to someone else.

03 / ONE OBJECT, MANY RELATIONS

Spend a moment with Hotei

The photograph shows the Met’s freshwater jar named Hotei, an Iga-ware vessel dated around 1600, with natural ash glaze. A mizusashi holds water to replenish the kettle. The museum describes this jar’s role in the deliberate combination of utensils at a gathering.

What catches your eye first: the uneven outline, or the colour moving across its surface? Imagine a smooth vessel beside it, then something with a similar colour. You need not reconstruct a tea ceremony to let one artwork prompt a closer look at relationships between objects.

04 / A SMALL TABLE EXPERIMENT

At home, start with two

For an everyday table, choose just two things you already use safely, perhaps a bowl and a tray. Look at colour today and the impression of texture another time. One point of attention makes comparison easier. There is no need to rearrange the whole room.

If you are having tea alone, you might photograph a combination you like. When inviting someone, think about ease of use as well. A secure grip for hot drinks and a stable base matter separately from appearance. Do not assume an antique is safe for food or drink from its photograph or atmosphere.

05 / KEEP THE CONVERSATION OPEN

Explain the choice, not a rule

These home experiments are KOTO editorial suggestions, not instructions for formal tea practice. At a tea gathering, follow the guidance of your host or teacher.

“I wanted to try these two together” can be enough to begin an everyday conversation. Before searching for another object, change the company your familiar ones keep. You may find a fresh reason to enjoy what is already there.

Ceramics

A Guide to Japan’s Ceramic Regions

A vessel can carry the name of a place. Knowing that place can change how we see a familiar plate: its clay, glaze and painted lines. This guide introduces 16 ceramic traditions, from Kanto to Okinawa.

This is a selection, not a complete national directory. Locations indicate principal centers, not every production area. The descriptions are starting points: styles vary between makers, kilns and periods.

Kanto

01

Mashiko, Tochigi

Mashiko ware

Mashiko developed through everyday vessels such as storage jars and grinding bowls. Its connections with Shoji Hamada and the mingei movement offer another way into its ceramics. Begin by comparing the forms and the character of the clay.

Source: 陶器の日・その他の産地 (Japanese) ↗
02

Kasama, Ibaraki

Kasama ware

Kasama's production has expanded from kitchen vessels to tableware and flower vessels. Rather than looking for one defining color or pattern, look for the forms and expressions of individual makers.

Source: 伝統工芸 青山スクエア・笠間焼 (Japanese) ↗

Chubu

03

Nomi, Kanazawa, Komatsu and Kaga, Ishikawa

Kutani ware

Rich overglaze decoration is one way into Kutani ware. Its well-known palette includes green, yellow, red, purple and deep blue, used in varied combinations and styles. Try looking at a vessel as you would a small painting.

Source: 陶器の日・九谷焼 (Japanese) ↗
04

Tono region, Gifu

Mino ware

Mino encompasses contrasting traditions including Shino, Oribe, Ki-Seto and Seto-guro. Its breadth resists a single visual definition. White, green, yellow and black glazes can provide a starting point for comparison.

Source: 国際陶磁器フェスティバル美濃・美濃焼とは (Japanese) ↗
05

Seto, Aichi

Seto ware

Seto produces a broad range of ceramics, from tableware to decorative objects. Akazu ware and Seto Sometsuke ware are also made within the region. Think of Seto as a place containing many techniques and kinds of work.

Source: 愛知県陶磁器工業協同組合・瀬戸焼について (Japanese) ↗
06

Tokoname and surrounding areas, Aichi

Tokoname ware

Tokoname is known for red-clay teapots, whose color comes from iron in the clay. Its production also includes glazed vessels and flower containers. Tea utensils offer a starting point for exploring different surfaces and forms.

Source: 伝統工芸 青山スクエア・常滑焼 (Japanese) ↗

Kinki

07

Yokkaichi and surrounding areas, Mie

Yokkaichi Banko ware

Banko ware is known for donabe cooking pots and purple-brown clay teapots. It connects ceramics with cooking and brewing as well as serving. Check each product's instructions for compatibility with direct heat or induction.

Source: 陶器の日・四日市萬古焼 (Japanese) ↗
08

Shigaraki, Shiga

Shigaraki ware

Shigaraki makes everything from tea vessels that emphasize the clay to tanuki figures and plant pots. Alongside its associations with unglazed ware, there are many expressions using glazes to discover.

Source: 陶器の日・信楽焼 (Japanese) ↗
09

Kyoto and surrounding areas

Kyo and Kiyomizu ware

Kyoto's ceramics developed within a wider craft culture, drawing on varied designs and techniques. Rather than defining the region by one clay or appearance, explore the details of individual makers' work.

Source: 陶器の日・京焼・清水焼 (Japanese) ↗

Chugoku

10

Bizen and surrounding areas, Okayama

Bizen ware

Bizen is fired without applied glaze or painted decoration. Ash, flame and placement in the kiln contribute to its surfaces. Even vessels of similar shape invite close comparison of their colors and markings.

Source: 伝統工芸 青山スクエア・備前焼 (Japanese) ↗
11

Hagi and surrounding areas, Yamaguchi

Hagi ware

Hagi ware is associated with a soft clay character and the tea ceremony. Its absorbency can cause colors to change with use. Ask the maker how a particular piece may change and how it should be cared for.

Source: 伝統工芸 青山スクエア・萩焼 (Japanese) ↗

Shikoku

12

Tobe, Ehime

Tobe ware

Substantial white porcelain with blue decoration offers a recognizable introduction to Tobe ware. Imagine it on your everyday table, and notice the weight, the shape of the rim and the rhythm of the pattern.

Source: 砥部町・砥部焼パンフレット（PDF） (Japanese) ↗

Kyushu

13

Arita and surrounding areas, Saga

Arita ware

Arita porcelain offers blue-and-white decoration as well as colorful overglaze painting on a white body. Some pieces leave generous open space; others build up patterns. Look at how decoration works with the vessel's shape.

Source: ピースクラフツSAGA・佐賀の伝統工芸 (Japanese) ↗
14

Karatsu and surrounding areas, Saga

Karatsu ware

Karatsu is associated with the character of its clay and a tradition of tea ceramics. Its varied forming and decorating techniques resist one fixed image. Let the texture of an individual piece guide your encounter.

Source: 伝統的工芸品産業振興協会・工芸品資料（唐津焼、PDF） (Japanese) ↗
15

Hasami, Nagasaki

Hasami ware

Hasami has long supplied everyday tableware. Alongside white porcelain and blue-and-white decoration, it offers designs for contemporary tables. Consider how a piece stacks and feels in the hand as you picture using it each day.

Source: 陶器の日・波佐見焼 (Japanese) ↗

Okinawa

16

Tsuboya in Naha, Yomitan and other areas, Okinawa

Tsuboya ware

Rooted in Tsuboya in Naha, this tradition is now also practiced beyond that district. It includes arayachi, associated with storage jars, and glazed joyachi, which encompasses bowls, plates and other everyday vessels.

Source: 壺屋焼窯元 育陶園・壺屋焼 (Japanese) ↗

Beyond the name of a place

Choose one place that catches your interest, then ask which maker created the vessel and what they intended. Comparing pieces made in the same area can turn a regional name into something you recognize through your own senses.

Descriptions of places and characteristics draw on the linked sources; suggestions for looking are KOTO editorial commentary. Sources checked on November 8, 2026. Opening hours and event dates are outside this guide’s scope.

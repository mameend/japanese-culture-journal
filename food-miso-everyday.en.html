FOOD / MISO AND EVERYDAY LIFE

One Small Tub of Miso, One Familiar Bowl

Rice, barley or soybean koji: a few words on the label can open up the world of miso. Start with a small tub and the soup you already like.

2026.11.09 / KOTO

01 / ONE SMALL TUB

Begin with tonight’s soup

At the miso shelf, look just beyond your usual choice. Pale cream, reddish brown, visible grains, a smooth paste: there is plenty to catch the eye. If the range feels daunting, picture a bowl of soup you would enjoy tonight. Tofu and wakame seaweed, perhaps, or daikon and aburaage, thin fried tofu. Familiar ingredients offer an easy place to begin.

Choose a small tub that fits the way you cook. Using one miso over several meals gives you time to discover what you like. Alongside the price, check the ingredients, whether dashi stock is already included, and the storage instructions.

02 / RICE, BARLEY, SOYBEANS

The names point to the koji

One useful distinction is the ingredient used to make koji. Rice miso, or kome miso, combines rice koji with soybeans. Barley miso, mugi miso, uses barley koji with soybeans. “Rice miso” does not mean that soybeans are absent.

For soybean miso, mame miso, the soybeans themselves become the koji. Blended miso brings different miso types or koji together. Reading these words on the label reveals something that colour alone cannot tell you: which ingredients underpin the paste.

03 / A PLACE ON THE LABEL

Let a place name draw you in

Rice miso is made across Japan; Shinshu miso is one regional example, associated with Nagano. Barley miso is particularly associated with Kyushu and parts of Chugoku and Shikoku, in western Japan. Soybean miso has its principal production areas in Aichi, Mie and Gifu. Ingredient proportions, maturation and regional preferences all contribute to this variety.

A place you have visited, or would like to know, can be a pleasant reason to try a tub. Regional names are starting points. Read the producer’s description of the particular product, too. A brief note of its name and origin can make the next choice easier.

04 / BEYOND THE COLOUR

Read the flavour words as well

Red, white and light-coloured miso are colour categories, distinct from rice, barley and soybean types. Ingredients, the way soybeans are cooked and the amount of koji can affect colour. Salt and the proportion of koji influence the balance of sweetness and saltiness. Edo sweet miso, for example, is reddish brown.

Look for flavour descriptions rather than predicting the taste from appearance. Amakuchi indicates a sweeter style; karakuchi describes a saltier one in this context, rather than chilli heat. If a shop can advise you, a simple request such as “a little sweeter than my usual miso” gives the conversation a useful starting point. Keep the flavour you already enjoy as your reference, and let curiosity guide the occasional small change to your everyday shopping.

05 / THE FAMILIAR BOWL

Find your preference over a few meals

When opening a new tub, try keeping the soup ingredients and stock familiar. Notice one thing as you eat: the aroma, sweetness, saltiness or the flavour that lingers. “I would like this again with daikon” is a perfectly useful tasting note.

This is a KOTO suggestion for everyday enjoyment. For quantities and cooking, follow the product’s guidance or a reliable recipe. Check allergen information, the best-before date and instructions for storage after opening. As you work through one small tub, your preferences may become clearer. The next visit to the miso shelf can then feel like something to look forward to.

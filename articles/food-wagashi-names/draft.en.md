# Reading the Seasons in Wagashi Names

A small label can open a seasonal landscape. Explore kamei, the individual names of wagashi, and enjoy a sweet through its name, colour and form.

## Read the little label again

When choosing wagashi, what catches your eye first: the colour, a delicate surface pattern, or a shape that seems made to fit in your palm? Once a sweet draws your attention, take a moment to read its label. A name you want to say aloud can be a lovely starting point for choosing today’s treat.

Beyond category names such as dango or daifuku, some wagashi have individual names called kamei. Japan’s Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries explains that these names can draw on poetry, seasonal nature and local history.

If you cannot read a name, ask the shop how to pronounce it. If an explanation is provided, spend a moment with it before eating. There is no need to remember everything. Sometimes the sound of a name is what stays with you.

## The landscape behind “Tatsuta”

One example introduced by the Japan Wagashi Association is Tatsuta, a name for a fresh confection expressing autumn foliage. The association traces it to the Tatsuta River in northwestern Nara Prefecture, known for its autumn leaves.

Knowing that connection, you might look at a sweet’s colours and imagine trees beside the water or leaves beginning to fall. This is KOTO’s suggestion for enjoying the experience. Even if you have never visited the place, finding it on a map can turn a tea break into a small excursion.

This is an example of a confection’s name, not a claim of current availability in shops. The sweet in our photograph has not been identified as Tatsuta.

## Season in colour and form

The ministry also explains that nerikiri can express different seasons through changes in colour and shape while using the same ingredients. Alongside a name, outlines and layers of colour can offer another way to notice the season.

At home, set your chosen sweet on a plate and look at it before taking a bite. Notice pale and dark areas, rounded and pointed lines. Your own words are enough to describe what feels seasonal. A small impression, perhaps “like today’s sky,” may make the name feel more personal.

## A small record of a sweet

Write a name you like in a notebook with the date. Keep the shop’s explanation separate from your own impressions, so you can enjoy both when you return to the page. If you take a photograph, you might place a note with the name beside the sweet. Afterwards, add a few words about its sweetness or texture.

Check the individual product’s label or the shop’s guidance for ingredients, allergens, storage and use-by information. Avoid assuming what a sweet contains or tastes like from its name alone.

These ways of looking and keeping notes are informal suggestions, not prescribed etiquette. Add a moment to read a name during your usual tea break. On your next visit to a shop, another word may catch your eye.

# Red, Black, and a Second Look at Negoro Lacquer

An old tea caddy invites us to look beyond an even finish. Explore lacquer, traces of use, and the pleasure of noticing an object without needing to own it.

## Another kind of beautiful

A new bowl with an even finish can be lovely. Yet an uneven colour can also hold our attention. Look at this small lidded vessel, with black appearing among the red. Before learning its name, give the photograph a moment.

Follow the rounded vertical ribs and the line where the lid meets the body. Where does the light gather? Where does the shape turn away? Looking closely makes “old” feel less like a single description and more like the beginning of several questions.

## Meeting Negoro lacquer

The Cleveland Museum of Art dates this Japanese tea caddy to the 1500s, in the Muromachi period, and identifies its material as Negoro lacquer on wood. The museum explains Negoro as red lacquer layered over a black undercoat, with years of handling revealing irregular red-and-black patterns.

A photograph cannot tell us every place the vessel has been or who held it. Keeping documented history separate from an imagined story leaves room for curiosity without turning a pleasing possibility into a fact.

## A vessel, but not a ceramic

This caddy has a wooden body, not a ceramic one. Urushi lacquer uses sap from the urushi tree. As the Japan Kogei Association explains, lacquer objects can have bodies of wood, paper, bamboo, metal or ceramics.

Imagine the red in this photograph beside matte paper or a white vessel. Which pairing would make you want to keep looking? This is a KOTO suggestion for enjoying the image, not a traditional arrangement rule. Leave weight and touch as open questions: a photograph cannot supply those sensations.

## At an antique shop, begin with a question

If you encounter a similar surface, you need not decide immediately that it is authentic Negoro or valuable because it is old. Ask what is known: the material, the basis for the dating, or whether repairs are documented. One unfamiliar word at a time is enough.

Your reason for liking it can be simpler. “I like the different reds” or “The rounded shape and narrow ribs catch my eye.” Having your own observation gives you somewhere to return as you listen to an explanation.

## You do not have to take it home

Enjoying an old object need not lead to buying it. Sketch its outline, or compare it with another lidded vessel in a museum collection online. Either is a small way to discover which forms and colours you enjoy.

Keep appreciation separate from practical use, too. Do not infer food suitability or appropriate handling from an antique’s appearance; ask a specialist who knows the object. The useful thing to take away from this caddy may be a slightly more attentive way of looking.

# Hojicha: A Pause for Roasted Tea

Follow hojicha’s roasted aroma to the work behind the tea, including Ishikawa’s stem-based Kaga bōcha, then bring that curiosity to an everyday break.

## Begin with the aroma

Between tasks, or beside a half-read book, make a cup of hojicha and give its aroma a moment before you drink. You need not find a precise description. A simple impression of something pleasantly toasted is enough to begin.

If you would like something sweet alongside it, choose a familiar favourite. Try the tea first, then a bite, then another sip. Notice how the same cup seems to you at each point. This is a small pleasure you can explore without special equipment or a vocabulary borrowed from a tasting room.

## What the name tells you

Japan’s Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries describes hojicha as tea made by roasting sencha, bancha and similar teas at high heat. The roasted aroma has a practical story behind it: a further stage of work on the tea.

Next time you choose a packet, look for a detail about its ingredients or roasting. If a seller asks what you enjoy, the name of a tea you already drink can be a useful answer. You can build a preference from one familiar cup rather than trying to learn every term at once.

## The stems in Kaga bōcha

One regional example is Kaga bōcha, a roasted stem tea associated with Ishikawa Prefecture. MAFF’s account follows its production through steaming, rolling and drying, then sorting the stems before roasting. Thinking about those slender pieces brings another part of the tea plant into view.

The Ishikawa tea trade cooperative describes a local tradition in which tea businesses roast bōcha using their own methods. If you encounter it while travelling, ask a shop about its approach. Back home, the shop’s name on the packet can be a small connection to the place, something to remember as you prepare another cup.

## Choose a roast to explore

MAFF’s introduction to Kaga bōcha distinguishes light, medium and deeper roasts. Even within one kind of tea, there are choices for the maker to make. Where a shop offers different roasts, small packets can turn choosing tea into a gentle comparison at home.

Start with each product’s own guidance for the amount of tea, water temperature and brewing time. Set the cups beside one another and notice the colour, the first aroma and the impression left after a sip. Which would you like on its own? Which would you reach for with your usual snack? You do not have to explain every difference to discover which cup invites you back.

## Leave a clue for next time

When you find one you like, keep a brief note of its name and how you brewed it. Next time, you might change the amount of water a little, or simply make it the same way again. These tasting and pairing ideas are KOTO’s suggestions for enjoyment, rather than formal tea etiquette.

For a few minutes, the ordinary act of making tea can bring to mind the people sorting stems and deciding how to roast them. Then return to your book or the work in front of you. A little knowledge of what is in the packet gives you something else to enjoy when the next break comes.
